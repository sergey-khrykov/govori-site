Govori

Frequently asked questions

What the dictionary is, where its data comes from, and what it costs — answered by the person who makes it.

Govori is a dictionary I built because I needed it myself.

I moved to Serbia, fell for the language, and found that the good material was either fragmentary, or trapped in scanned books, or wrapped in an interface from 2004. So I started building the dictionary I wanted: every word form and its accent, search that understands whatever form you actually heard, no ads, no account, and no internet required. It's a one-person project, made with a lot of help from published reference dictionaries and a lot of stubbornness.

18 questions

The dictionary

What is Govori?

Govori is a family of modern learner's dictionaries. It's built around the things paper dictionaries never had room for and most apps don't bother with: complete grammar — every case, every tense, with the accent marked — search that recognises the inflected form you actually heard, and entries you can read on a phone in a shop queue.

It's fully offline: the whole dictionary ships inside the app, so lookups are instant and work in a basement, on a plane, or with a dead SIM. No ads, no account, no tracking of what you look up.

Who is it for?

Anyone who learns the language or has to live in it. Expats getting through paperwork, students, immigrants, business travellers, heritage speakers who grew up hearing the language but never studied its grammar, translators, teachers.

It's built for both directions of the problem: understanding what you read or heard, and producing a form correctly yourself — which is where the grammar tables earn their place.

Why should I install it?

You don't have to. But if you use the language in real life, or you're learning it, you might want to: full grammar for every entry, search that forgives diacritics, scripts and word forms, no ads, and it all works offline.

The honest test: look up a word you struggled with this week. If the entry tells you something you couldn't get in ten seconds elsewhere, the app is for you.

How it came about

Why build it?

I lived in Serbia as an immigrant. I was fascinated by the language, and frustrated by how little modern support it had. Wonderful sources exist — Wiktionary, the standard reference dictionaries, PDFs that circulate among learners — but using them meant juggling five tabs and a scanned book, and none of them was built for the moment you actually need a word.

At some point I got tired of that and decided to build a dictionary with a genuinely good experience for the way we live now: offline, fast, smart about search, complete on grammar, free of ads.

I believe that knowing the local language is the key to belonging anywhere in the world. This is my attempt to close part of that gap — and a small homage to the languages and dialects of the Balkans.

What does the name mean?

Govori is a Serbian word you meet on day one. It's both the imperative — "speak!" — and the third person: "he speaks", "she speaks". A dictionary should be an invitation, not a monument, so that seemed right.

The Slovene app is called Lahko — "easily", and also the word that turns a verb into "you can": lahko govoriš, "you can speak". Same idea, other language.

Who built it?

Hi — I'm Sergei, an immigrant from Russia. I spent two years in Serbia learning the language, and I now live in Slovenia, where I'm building the Slovene app for exactly the same reason.

I'm a software engineer, not a trained lexicographer, and that shapes how the dictionary is made: I don't invent language facts. Everything in it traces back to a published reference dictionary or a documented source, and the app can tell you which one.

Sources and method

Where does the data come from?

In its early days Govori was meant to be a nice interface over Wiktionary — a free, genuinely impressive dictionary that already covers a lot of the language. I learned quickly that this doesn't work. Wiktionary's coverage is uneven: strong in places, thin exactly where everyday words live, and often silent about which regional standard a form belongs to. It's a great skeleton and a poor final answer.

So Govori uses its own approach to choosing words, writing entries and checking them. Wiktionary is still the starting skeleton, it's credited in the app, and its licence travels with it — but the dictionary is no longer a copy of it. Everything that matters is verified against published reference dictionaries and language portals: Matica srpska and Hrvatski jezični portal for BCMS, Fran and the Sloleks database for Slovene, among others.

So how exactly is it built?

Two steps: decide which words to add, then write and verify them.

The word list comes from three places:

  1. Frequency rankings from language corpora — the words people actually use, most-used first. This is what keeps the project finite.
  2. Simulated real-life situations — going to a doctor, renting a flat, registering a residence. These surface words that corpora under-represent but learners need immediately.
  3. You — what people search for and don't find, plus direct reports when someone knows a word exists and it isn't there yet.

Then the writing. This is where language models do the heavy lifting: reviewing existing entries, drafting new ones, checking the accents and paradigm cells. They are not allowed to answer from memory. Every claim has to be transcribed from a named reference that actually prints it, and each entry stores a record of what was checked — including the references that were consulted and did not have the word. A claim with nothing behind it never reaches the app.

It isn't magic and it isn't perfect. It is, however, checkable — which is more than I could say for my own guesses.

How is Govori different from HJP, Matica srpska, or the classic bilingual dictionaries?

It isn't a replacement for any of them. It's built on top of them, links out to them, and would be far worse without them.

They're different kinds of object:

  • Matica srpska and the other standard reference dictionaries are the authority on the Serbian norm. They're also books: you can't search them by an inflected form you heard on the street, and they don't hand you a full conjugation on demand.
  • Hrvatski jezični portal is an excellent free portal for the Croatian norm — online, Croatian-facing, and not built for someone whose starting language is English or Russian.
  • The classic bilingual dictionaries (Stanković, Tolstoy and others) are superb works of scholarship, frozen at the year of their edition and shaped by paper.
  • Most dictionary apps are word lists with a translation and an ad banner.

Govori's specific job is to be the learner's layer: every form, accented, in one place, searchable by whatever you actually heard, offline, with the sources named — and where the standards disagree, saying who says what instead of silently picking one.

Where does Govori stand on "one language or four"?

Out of the argument, deliberately. I'm a guest in these languages, and it isn't my quarrel to adjudicate.

What I do instead is practical. There's one shared body of evidence behind the BCMS apps, and each individual form records which standards prescribe it, merely tolerate it, or reject it — judged against that standard's own authority, not my taste. Separate apps exist so that the standard you use is the default you see: someone in Podgorica shouldn't have to mentally filter Croatian endings out of every table, and someone in Zagreb shouldn't get Belgrade's.

Nothing is deleted for belonging to a neighbour. Where the standards diverge, the other form stays visible and labelled, because a learner deserves to know that a choice exists — that's information, not politics.

Money

Why isn't it completely free?

A lot of it is, permanently: search, meanings in English and Russian, usage examples, favourites and history. No ads on any of it, and nothing about your lookups leaves your phone.

What's paid is the depth — the grammar tables. Those are the part that takes years: a paradigm isn't scraped, it's verified cell by cell against reference works, and thousands of them have been corrected by hand.

I have no investors, I don't sell data, and I refuse to sell your attention to an ad network. That leaves one honest option: the people who get the most out of the dictionary pay for it, and their money pays for the next ten thousand words.

How is it priced?

Everything is unlocked for 14 days after you install — the full app, no card, no account. After that, lookups, meanings and examples stay free forever, and the grammar tables become part of Govori Plus.

Plus comes in three shapes, and the app shows the current price in your own currency:

  • Pečenje — one payment, yours forever. The best value and the one I'd pick.
  • Sarma — a yearly subscription.
  • Kafa — monthly, cancel whenever you like.

The food names are decorative; every option always shows exactly what it bills and when. There are no countdown-timer tricks and nothing renews without saying so first. One purchase covers every feature that ships afterwards.

Why offer a subscription at all?

Honestly, I'd rather sell you the app once — that's why the one-time purchase is the headline option and the best deal in the list.

The subscription exists for two reasons. Some people don't want to commit up front to something they've used for two weeks, and a year or a month is a fairer way to try it. And the dictionary isn't finished: words get added, mistakes get corrected, new features ship. That work continues every month, so some revenue that continues every month keeps it healthy.

Can I donate?

There's no tip jar. If you want to support the project, buy the one-time purchase — that is the donation, and it gets you the whole dictionary. If you already own it, telling one person who's learning the language does more for it than money.

What's next

Will there be audio? Flashcards?

Both are planned, and I won't promise dates.

Audio is the most requested feature by a wide margin, and the design is done: pronunciation for core vocabulary bundled with the app for everyone, fuller coverage and slower playback as part of Plus.

Learning is coming as word lists you build yourself — save words from any entry, then practise them. Practising will stay free; that's not a thing I want to put behind a paywall. Alongside it, themed vocabulary for real situations, built from the same "what do you actually need at the bank" thinking that shapes the word list.

Why English and Russian — and will there be other languages?

English is the language most learners arrive with, so it was never a question.

Russian is there for two reasons. The honest one: it's my own first language, and I was building the dictionary I needed. The better one: Russian speakers are one of the largest groups of newcomers to Serbia and Montenegro, and there was nothing modern serving them.

There's also a linguistic reason that turned out to matter more than I expected. Russian is not a second-rate English here. It's a Slavic language, so it already has the categories Serbian needs — verbal aspect, cases that govern each other, reflexive verbs, participles. A Russian gloss can name a perfective pair in one word where English needs a whole clause and still loses the distinction. If you speak a Slavic language, that's a shortcut English simply can't give you.

Which is why the Russian side isn't a translation of the English side. It's written independently, against the Serbian word itself, and the two sometimes divide a word's meanings differently — because sometimes they genuinely should.

Next up: French is first in line, driven mostly by heritage speakers in France who'd rather learn through their own language than through English. German and Italian are the ones asked for after that. The plan is that you'll download only the language you use, so adding languages never inflates anyone else's app.

Nothing here is promised on a date, and the order isn't fixed. If you want a particular language, tell me — that's literally how the list gets sorted.

Are other languages of the region coming?

Slovene already exists as Lahko — a separate app with its own dictionary, built from Slovene sources rather than adapted from Serbian. Montenegrin, Croatian and Bosnian editions of Govori are in preparation, each defaulting to its own standard for the same reasons as above.

Beyond that I make no promises. Every language in this family is a year of somebody's life, and I'd rather do four properly than twelve badly.

Getting involved

Can I help?

Yes, please, and the most useful help is the smallest:

  • Report a missing word or a wrong form from inside the app. These go straight into the work queue, and "I'm sure this word exists" reports are genuinely valuable.
  • Join the Telegram group to argue with me about accents and hear what's coming.
  • Teachers, translators, lexicographers, corpus people: write to support@govoridictionaries.com. If you teach the language and want it in your classroom, tell me — I'd like to make that easy.
  • Leave a review. For an app with no marketing budget, that's oxygen.
